# Satellite screening of lighting change across Australia's protected areas

## Abstract

Annual satellite night-lights allow protected areas to be screened repeatedly for changes in artificial lighting, but low radiance, small reserves and complex boundaries constrain their use in conservation. We assessed VIIRS-derived annual radiance for 2013–2024 across Australia's 2024 protected-area estate, comparing reserve interiors with surrounding unprotected land and, where raster support permitted, with bands inside reserve boundaries. Of 11,133 protected areas, 4,426 (39.8%) met the pixel and temporal eligibility rules; although fewer than half by number, they account for 99.8% of summed reserve area. Among these, 3,844 (86.9%) had zero inside mean radiance in 2024, and 420 (9.5%) had positive full-period Theil–Sen slopes. A zero slope did not necessarily indicate an unchanging series: 180 of the 3,969 zero-slope areas had observed annual variation, including 78 with positive observations after 2021. A product audit found a synchronous pulse of first-positive annual observations in 2017 and increases in 2023 across inside and surrounding cohorts, so processing effects on these recent detections remain unresolved. Among 4,253 areas with paired inside and 1 km surrounding trends, 347 had both slopes positive, 70 only the inside slope positive and 249 only the surrounding slope positive; an inside-only screen would have missed this last group. Of 150 positive-trend reserves with usable deeper-interior data, 42 retained a positive slope in the 1–5 km interior band; most others were positive in the outermost kilometre but not in that band. The assessment provides a national baseline for conservation screening that keeps spatial eligibility, product non-detection and annual trajectories in view. Its transferable lesson is to use satellite trend categories to direct local investigation, relying on product-compatibility checks and annual observations to interpret changes that full-period slopes can miss.

## Keywords

artificial light at night; protected areas; VIIRS; remote sensing; conservation monitoring

## 1. Introduction

Protected-area managers need repeatable information about pressures that extend beyond reserve boundaries. Artificial light at night (ALAN) is one such pressure: lighting can alter behaviour, physiology and species interactions, and its influence is not confined to the land on which it originates [@longcore2004; @gaston2013; @hirt2023]. Satellite night-lights provide broad spatial coverage and a growing temporal record, which makes them attractive for screening reserve estates that cannot be surveyed comprehensively from the ground. Since 2012, the day/night band (DNB) of the Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) has recorded upward radiance at night with a ground footprint of about 750 m. Annual composites of these data average cloud-free observations after removing dim background noise and transient sources such as fires [@elvidge2021].

Previous studies have documented increasing lighting within and around protected areas [@gaston2015; @guette2018; @mu2021], and skyglow, the diffuse brightening of the night sky by scattered artificial light, reaches into conservation-priority areas [@garrett2020]. Translating those observations into a monitoring system requires decisions about measurement support, surrounding-land comparisons and the meaning of a trend. On the nominal 500 m product grid, some reserves contain too few pixels for a stable summary, and in low-radiance landscapes the annual composites contain many zeros. A buffer drawn around a reserve may also include ocean or neighbouring protected tenure, neither of which represents the adjacent unprotected landscape. Such choices influence both the apparent environmental pattern and which reserves an agency can assess at all.

Satellite upward radiance also differs from sky brightness and from the illumination experienced by organisms, because spectral sensitivity, atmospheric propagation and changing lighting technologies separate these quantities [@falchi2016; @kyba2023; @levin2025; @bara2025]. A satellite-based dark-sky indicator therefore measures one particular aspect of reserve condition and cannot certify ecological darkness. The monitoring task is to identify where the measured signal merits investigation and where the product lacks adequate support.

Temporal summaries raise a further distinction. A positive slope from a robust estimator is useful for screening long-term change, but a zero slope can occur in a series that contains recent or episodic positive values. Similarly, a reserve whose interior and surroundings are both brightening poses different follow-up questions from one where the increase is confined to a single zone, although neither pattern reveals a lighting source. Retaining the annual values alongside any category keeps these distinctions visible to managers.

We assessed the Australian protected-area estate using annual VIIRS-derived night-lights for 2013–2024. Three questions guided the analysis: which reserves have sufficient raster support to be assessed; how full-period trends inside reserves compare with trends in adjacent unprotected land; and what information is lost when annual observations are reduced to the sign of a slope. Interior bands and marginal temporal-resampling summaries supplied additional diagnostics. The main contribution is a worked national application of satellite screening for conservation, in which eligibility and temporal interpretation are carried through to reserve-level examples.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1 Protected-area estate and night-lights product

We used the 2024 Collaborative Australian Protected Areas Database (CAPAD), dissolving polygons by stable protected-area identifier (`PA_ID`) and repairing invalid geometries. The resulting 11,133 identifiers were the analytical units, and distance and area calculations used the Australian equal-area projection EPSG:3577. Because the fixed 2024 estate was assessed retrospectively across all years, the analysis does not compare contemporaneous annual reserve boundaries or estimate an effect of designation.

Annual inputs were the 2013–2024 VIIRS-derived Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs in the Hengl/OpenGeoHub night-lights dataset, version 0.4 [@hengl2025nightlights], which follows the Annual VIIRS Nighttime Lights (VNL) V2 methodology [@elvidge2021]. The derivative dataset rescales the source values and also includes earlier, extrapolated years; we used only its 2013–2024 annual layers. Each GeoTIFF's scale and offset were applied, including the scale-metadata change in 2022–2024. Source tags retained in the files identify the 2022 input as a V2.2 masked composite combining Suomi-NPP and NOAA-20 observations, and the 2023–2024 inputs as Suomi-NPP masked composites. The earlier archived processing script supports V2.1 masked inputs for 2013–2021 but does not fully reproduce the later deposit's encoding (Supporting Methods S2). Throughout, *product-scaled radiance* means these metadata-adjusted values; we report them in scaled product units and do not convert them to physical radiance, ground illuminance or sky brightness.

In every year from 2013 to 2024, the smallest positive decoded pixel value in the Australian audit window was 10 product units. This observed floor applies to individual pixels; spatial means, which average positive and zero pixels, can be smaller. Integer quantisation and upstream masking both constrain low-radiance observations, and their separate contributions remain unresolved. Zeros were retained as product non-detections, without imputation or smoothing, while negative source codes and no-data values were excluded. Missing measurements remained missing, and annual layers were neither interpolated nor substituted across years. Source versions and checksums were preserved, and a targeted continuity audit compared stored pixel values, metadata-adjusted distributions and first-positive annual observations across the 2022 source boundary.

### 2.2 Spatial summaries and eligibility

For each protected area and year, we calculated the inside mean radiance, median, 95th percentile and fraction of valid pixels greater than zero. Raster extraction used centre-based polygon selection (`all_touched=False`), so a cell was included only when its centre fell within the polygon, and summaries of the selected valid cells were unweighted. Mean radiance was the primary intensity summary. The other metrics describe the distribution, particularly its zero mass, and were not used as alternative outcomes from which stronger trends could be selected.

We constructed 1 and 5 km outward buffers and compared raw geometric buffers, land-masked buffers and land buffers excluding overlap with all protected areas. The last, termed the *unprotected-land buffer*, was the primary surrounding comparison. Removing ocean and protected tenure defines the adjacent unprotected landscape more consistently, although it does not locate the source of incoming light. The 1 km buffer supplied the main screening comparison and the 5 km buffer a wider view of the surroundings.

Land masking used Australian Coastline 50K 2024 version 1-1 polygons [@hammerton2024coastline], which are derived from 2022–2024 Sentinel-2 imagery and approximate mean high water. Six small reserve polygons with no intersection with this land source were excluded from land-dependent surrounding comparisons; none belonged to the eligible inside cohort. Buffers and inward bands retained the repaired CAPAD boundaries without simplification. Circular arcs used 128 segments per quadrant, giving a maximum chord error below 0.19 m at 10 km, and a raster-support convergence check is described in Supporting Methods S3. Distances are projected EPSG:3577 distances rather than exact geodesic distances. Comparisons near documented coastline-source exceptions were flagged as provisional in the reserve-level results and require local map validation before site use (Supporting Methods S3).

Every zone-year summary required at least 10 valid pixels at the product's nominal 500 m resolution, and values failing this threshold were treated as missing. Trend estimation also required at least eight valid years, both inside reserves and in their surroundings. These rules favour reserves that are large and compact enough for raster extraction, so eligibility is reported separately for each analysis. Pixel counts indicate raster support; they are not independent observations of ecological exposure.

Interior bands extended 0–1, 1–5 and 5–10 km inward from reserve boundaries. We report the feasibility of each band and describe 2024 patterns among areas meeting the pixel rule in all three, treating this complete-band subset as a geometry-selected diagnostic. Because a whole-reserve mean can rise when only cells near the boundary brighten, a separate sensitivity analysis recalculated slopes for the 1–5 km band under the same pixel and temporal rules. It compared whole-reserve and band categories only among areas eligible for both, holding the corrected 1 km surrounding slope fixed. A later review-stage check added 0–1 km slopes and annual zero status for the same cohort. To assess the magnitudes underlying the area-weighted summaries, we also grouped the 420 positive whole-reserve slopes into terciles and summed polygon areas within each.

### 2.3 Full-period trends and screening categories

Theil–Sen slopes, also called Sen slopes, summarised 2013–2024 change in annual mean product-scaled radiance. Each is the median of the slopes between all pairs of valid years, so a single anomalous year has little influence on the estimate. For areas with eligible inside and 1 km unprotected-land trends, we defined four descriptive categories: both slopes positive (Q1), only the inside slope positive (Q2), neither slope positive (Q3) and only the surrounding slope positive (Q4). Non-positive means zero or negative, so Q3 includes dimming. The inside-minus-surrounding slope difference was retained as a descriptive contrast. Categories used strict point-estimate signs, without a significance or minimum-effect threshold, and therefore describe only the direction of the full-period median pairwise slope. They do not describe the timing, cause or ecological importance of change; a non-positive surrounding slope, for example, does not imply that the final annual value is no higher than the first.

To distinguish zero slopes from unchanging observations, we examined the annual series of every eligible area with an exactly zero inside slope. We counted series with any variation among valid annual means, series with all observed values zero, and constant non-zero series. A separate late-positive pattern was defined explicitly: all 12 annual values valid, values in 2013–2021 zero, and at least one positive value in 2022–2024. This descriptive check was added during manuscript review and did not redefine the trend categories or test for ecological onset.

Because the late-positive window begins with a change in upstream processing, we also examined the 2022 product boundary directly. For inside reserves and both surrounding buffers, the annual first-positive fraction was the proportion of complete, previously all-zero series that recorded their first positive value in that year. Inside-reserve fractions were stratified by fixed distance from positive 2013 raster-cell centres, which keeps subsequent detections out of the reference mask. These comparisons are descriptive compatibility diagnostics and cannot identify the cause of change (Supporting Methods S2).

Four named reserves were retained to illustrate annual trajectories: Neerabup (WA), Henderson (WA), Black Mountain (ACT) and Bungonia (NSW). The selection is illustrative rather than representative, and Black Mountain was included specifically to show that Q3 can contain declining radiance. For each reserve, we plotted all annual inside and surrounding values and reported the full-period slopes.

### 2.4 Temporal sensitivity

Archived per-area resampling summaries were retained as a diagnostic of temporal sensitivity. Each selected area received independently generated moving-block resamples of its annual year-value pairs, using blocks of three consecutive annual observations to preserve short-run dependence between adjacent years. Slopes were recomputed and pooled by area. We report marginal medians, 2.5th–97.5th percentiles and fractions of resampled slopes greater than zero. The original procedure sampled regions of the Interim Biogeographic Regionalisation for Australia (IBRA) with replacement within each of 300 outer iterations. Because an area was resampled whenever its dominant region was drawn, the number of resamples varied among areas. These marginal distributions provide neither joint spatial uncertainty nor calibrated confidence intervals; Supporting Methods S1 describes the algorithm and its interpretation.

### 2.5 Reproducibility

The Python pipeline uses versioned source identities, pinned dependencies, stable output keys and run manifests. After independent review identified coarse buffer geometry and an unsuitable land mask, all surrounding and interior-band observations were re-extracted for 2013–2024. Geometry-only and coastline changes were compared separately (Supporting Methods S3). Whole-reserve annual observations were retained with exact keyed equality checks, and their trend estimates were verified. The corrected analysis has its own input and output manifest and does not overwrite the earlier archived baseline. Publicly archived processed outputs and the remaining boundaries on code and data access are specified below.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Assessment feasibility and 2024 radiance

Of 11,133 protected areas, 4,426 (39.8%) met the inside pixel and temporal eligibility rules (Table 1; Fig. 1). Paired inside and 1 km unprotected-land trends were available for 4,253 areas and the corresponding 5 km comparison for 4,370. These result-specific denominators reflect reserve geometry, surrounding-land masking and raster support. Compared with the earlier coarse-support implementation, 407 reserves gained paired eligibility and 12 lost it, while 57 of the 3,846 reserves common to both versions changed sign category (Table S5).

Among the 4,426 eligible areas, 3,844 (86.9%) had zero inside mean radiance in 2024; the remaining 582 were spatially concentrated near developed coastal and urban regions (Fig. 1). Area weighting gives a different impression. Eligible identifiers contained 99.8% of summed polygon area, and reserves with positive whole-reserve slopes contained 37.6% of eligible summed area, compared with 9.5% by count (Table 1). That share rests almost entirely on small slopes in large reserves. The 140 reserves in the lowest slope tercile, all with slopes below 0.04 product units per year, contributed 99.1% of the positive class's summed area (Table S4), and the other 280 contained only 0.32% of eligible summed area. The 0.04 cutoff describes the observed tercile division rather than an ecological-effect threshold. These area summaries weight each reserve by its full polygon area: overlapping designations may be counted more than once, and the area assigned to a trend class is not the area actually illuminated or changing.

Interior support declined with distance from the boundary. In 2024, the 0–1 km band was valid for 4,426 areas, the 1–5 km band for 1,398, and all three bands for only 369 (3.3% of the estate). Within this complete-band subset, the shares with any positive pixels were 17.62%, 8.94% and 3.52%, respectively (Fig. S1), and median fractions of positive pixels were zero in every band. The observed decline is conditional on this geometry-selected subset.

### 3.2 Full-period slopes and information concealed by zeros

Inside slopes were positive for 420 eligible areas (9.5%), negative for 37 (0.8%) and exactly zero for 3,969 (89.7%; Fig. 2). The mean across all eligible areas was 0.0283 scaled product units per year (SD 0.295). Among the 457 non-zero slopes, the median was 0.098 and the interquartile range 0.0075–0.4164. Marginal temporal resampling produced narrow ranges for some selected slopes and wide ranges for others (Fig. S2; Table S1). These ranges describe sensitivity to the resampling procedure and were used neither to assign categories nor to infer a reliable national ordering of reserves.

All 3,969 zero-slope areas had complete 12-year series. Most (3,786) had zero annual means throughout and three had constant non-zero means, but 180 (4.5%) showed annual variation despite their zero slopes. Seventy-eight of these (2.0% of zero-slope areas) met the late-positive definition, with nine zero-valued years followed by at least one positive annual value during 2022–2024. The zero-slope count therefore exceeds the number of unchanging observed series and cannot be interpreted as an absence of measured change.

The timing of these late detections complicates their interpretation. First-positive fractions rose synchronously in 2017 and 2023 in the inside cohort and in both surrounding cohorts (Fig. S3; Table S2). Inside first detections numbered 53 in 2017, 14 in 2022, 31 in 2023 and 33 in 2024. Thus 64 of the 78 late-positive series began in 2023–2024, after the year of the source change. Among audit-window pixels valid in every year from 2020 to 2024, zero-to-positive transitions doubled from 28,035 in 2022 to 57,328 in 2023, while positive-to-zero transitions fell from 21,520 to 12,031. Although there was no first-detection spike in 2022 itself, these calendar patterns leave processing effects unresolved.

### 3.3 Inside and surrounding trends

Among the 4,253 paired areas, both slopes were positive in 347 (Q1; 8.2%), only the inside slope was positive in 70 (Q2; 1.6%), neither slope was positive in 3,587 (Q3; 84.3%) and only the surrounding slope was positive in 249 (Q4; 5.9%; Fig. 3). Of the 596 areas with positive surrounding slopes, 249 would therefore have been missed by a screen based on inside trends alone. The four illustrative trajectories show how these categories translate into different temporal patterns (Fig. 4). At Neerabup, inside and surrounding slopes were both positive, at 8.150 and 3.847 scaled product units per year. Its inside annual mean nearly tripled, from 68.814 in 2013 to 190.339 in 2024. Henderson was also in Q1, with inside and surrounding slopes of 1.731 and 2.833. Its surrounding classification changed from non-positive to positive when spatial support was corrected, which shows how sensitive a coastal site's interpretation can be to land masking. At Black Mountain, inside and surrounding slopes were both negative (-1.037 and -3.952), so its Q3 classification denotes two non-positive trends rather than a stable or unlit site. Bungonia had an inside mean of zero throughout, while its surrounding series varied and had a positive slope of 0.417.

The 1–5 km interior-band sensitivity was feasible for 1,341 of the 4,253 paired reserves (31.5%). Within this matched subset, 27 of 112 Q1 reserves retained a positive interior-band slope, and the other 85 became Q4 when the band replaced the whole-reserve mean. Similarly, 15 of 38 Q2 reserves retained a positive band slope and 23 became Q3. All 85 Q1 losses had exactly zero 1–5 km slopes, although only 73 had all-zero band histories; the other 12 contained positive annual observations. The 1,103 original Q3 and 88 Q4 reserves retained their categories (Table S3). In total, 42 of 150 positive whole-reserve trends extended to a positive trend in this deeper band.

All 1,341 matched reserves also supplied 0–1 km slopes, and most of the remaining positive trends appeared there instead. Of the 150 positive whole-reserve cases, 100 had positive 0–1 km slopes and zero 1–5 km slopes, while 38 had positive slopes in both bands (Table S4). Measured dimming followed the same pattern, with negative 0–1 km slopes in 11 of the 12 matched reserves whose whole-reserve slopes were negative. Nine of those 11 had zero 1–5 km slopes and two had negative slopes, while the twelfth reserve had zero slopes in both bands.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 What the satellite screen contributes to conservation monitoring

This assessment identifies where annual night-lights can support reserve-level screening and makes the exclusions visible, so that reserves the product cannot assess are not mistaken for reserves without change. Its practical contribution lies in combining a fixed protected-area estate, explicit raster-support rules, corrected surrounding-land comparisons and inspectable annual trajectories. Together these form a baseline that agencies can revisit as compatible observations become available. The results complement earlier protected-area lighting assessments [@gaston2015; @guette2018; @garrett2020; @mu2021]. What transfers to other estates is the monitoring design, which involves defining the estate and its spatial support, inspecting product non-detection, distinguishing inside from surrounding change and retaining the observations underlying each trend. The Australian results show why such a sequence is needed: a single national brightening percentage would conceal the eligibility gaps, zero-slope variation and surrounding-only change reported here.

The paired comparison adds information that an inside-only trend map cannot. The 249 areas with positive surrounding but non-positive inside slopes warrant examination of change in the adjacent unprotected landscape, even though their inside trend category would not flag brightening. Areas with both slopes positive raise questions about conditions within the reserve as well as around it. Correcting buffers for ocean and protected tenure makes these comparisons more consistent across coastal reserves and neighbouring protected areas. Henderson shows that, for a coastal reserve, the choice of land mask can decide the category.

### 4.2 A zero slope is an incomplete monitoring result

The distinction between a zero annual value and a zero temporal slope has practical consequences. An annual zero indicates non-detection in the selected product, whereas a zero Theil–Sen slope is the median of pairwise rates of change and can arise despite positive annual observations. Nine zero values in a 12-year series, for example, generate 36 zero pairwise slopes out of 66, enough to yield a zero median even if all three later values increase.

In these data the limitation is concrete: 180 zero-slope areas had varying annual means, and 78 showed the specified late-positive pattern. Their existing categories need not be discarded, because those categories answer a full-period trend question. Monitoring late or intermittent product detections, however, requires reading the annual series or adding a separately defined temporal flag. The synchronous 2017 pulse and 2023 increases caution against interpreting such flags as environmental onset. The mixed-sensor 2022 composite could have suppressed some faint detections before the 2023 increase, but that is a possible processing explanation rather than an established cause. Persistence into subsequent years does not distinguish processing from environmental change either. The 78 cases therefore identify first-positive product observations for investigation, not confirmed arrivals of lighting. A trend screen and a recent-change alert answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable products.

Sign categories hide other variation too. Black Mountain demonstrates that the large Q3 category contains dimming as well as zeros, two outcomes that call for different interpretations. Retaining annual observations helps distinguish such trajectories and reveals fluctuations that a single slope sign conceals, which favours plain category names and ready access to the underlying series.

### 4.3 From a national flag to local investigation

A useful agency workflow begins by establishing whether a reserve and its surroundings are evaluable. For an eligible site, interpretation of a temporal flag should start with a check for product-version or processing boundaries in the series. The annual values and sign category come next, followed by the magnitude and temporal sensitivity of the trend and the local conservation context. At Neerabup, the paired increase directs attention to both spatial zones, whereas at Bungonia the first follow-up concerns the surrounding landscape and whether changes below satellite detection are occurring inside. These are pathways for investigation, not claims about the sources of light at either reserve.

Ground-based sky-brightness measurements, lighting inventories and information on facilities, roads, development and terrain can distinguish explanations that national radiance summaries cannot. Site priority would also depend on ecological sensitivity, conservation objectives and the practical scope for intervention, none of which the magnitude of a satellite slope can supply. The percentile ranges in Table S1 help readers inspect the sensitivity of selected estimates, but they are not formal confidence statements or a basis for competitive ranking.

Product zeros also require careful interpretation. Non-detection across a large reserve may coexist with ecologically relevant illumination at a small facility or boundary, and, conversely, satellite brightening does not demonstrate a harmful biological response. Opportunistic occurrence records cannot resolve that additional inference without accounting for sampling processes that can confound ecological interpretation [@phillips2009; @fithian2015; @baker2024]. We recommend standardised repeated surveys with suitable detection, effort and habitat information to investigate biological consequences.

### 4.4 Limits and transfer to future assessments

The study's scope is constrained first by its measurement product. VIIRS-derived upward radiance does not measure ground illumination, spectral composition or sky brightness, and changing lighting technologies can alter the relationships among these quantities [@falchi2016; @kyba2023; @levin2025; @bara2025]. A switch to white light-emitting diodes (LEDs), for instance, can reduce DNB radiance without reducing visible illumination, because the sensor is insensitive below approximately 500 nm [@kyba2017]. Black Mountain's declining product radiance therefore does not establish that the reserve became darker, nor does it identify an LED conversion. At the low end, the observed 10-unit pixel floor shows that values are discretised. Zeros may reflect both upstream masking and derivative encoding, whose separate contributions cannot be recovered from the deposited workflow, so the 86.9% zero share is not a calibrated measure of ecological darkness. Applying metadata reconciles the observed integer encodings but does not eliminate upstream processing, atmospheric or spectral influences. Spatial mixing adds a further limit, because resampling an approximately 750 m DNB footprint onto a nominal 500 m grid does not resolve lighting sources at reserve boundaries. Neither robust slopes nor temporal resampling recovers changes below detection.

Second, eligibility is selective: 60.2% of protected-area identifiers lacked sufficient support for the main assessment. National frequency statements therefore concern the eligible subset, in which small and narrow reserves are under-represented. Eligible reserves contain nearly all summed polygon area, but that does not remove the monitoring gap for the numerous small reserves. Counts of protected-area identifiers should not be read as area-weighted exposure or as statistically independent ecological replicates. Interior evidence is thinner still. The 369 complete-band areas cannot establish a national boundary-to-interior effect, and in the matched-band sensitivity most positive whole-reserve slopes did not remain positive 1–5 km inward. The 100 cases with positive 0–1 km but zero 1–5 km slopes support spatial concentration of the measured trend near the boundary within this cohort. Pixel mixing, product non-detection and change in other parts of a reserve still limit interpretation. The sensitivity neither identifies spillover nor replaces the national screen. The coastline source also has documented bridge and estuary errors, and affected surrounding comparisons require local map checks (Supporting Methods S3).

Third, the sign categories are descriptive, and small positive estimates qualify alongside larger ones. Because the positive class's large summed area is carried overwhelmingly by its lowest slope tercile, the 37.6% area share cannot be interpreted as widespread change of substantial magnitude. The marginal resampling ranges do not propagate measurement error, boundary uncertainty or spatial covariance, so formal statements about joint category confidence or reserve ordering would require a different inferential analysis.

Finally, the fixed CAPAD 2024 estate means that historical observations refer to land protected in that snapshot, including any land designated later in the study period. Future updates should record the identities of both the boundary data and the night-lights product, so that changes in the monitoring estate can be separated from environmental change. With these conditions made explicit, annual satellite observations can support repeatable conservation screening, identifying useful places to investigate while retaining the temporal and spatial information needed to interpret each flag.
